The "Stories Behind Accounting Standards" series has been published on my WeChat public account, on and off, over the course of a little more than a month — eleven articles in total — and it is now drawing to a close.

Writing this series grew out of a long-held wish of mine: to turn accounting — a subject widely regarded as dry — into stories, to make it interesting and engaging so that more people would want to learn about it. I also hoped that ordinary investors would pay more attention to financial statements, learn to read them, and gain a little more clarity in the capital markets.

My first job was at an accounting firm, where I stayed for many years. In the early years I even spent a few years "warming the bench" — even after busy season ended, life hardly eased up, because every spare moment was consumed by the monk-like discipline of studying for the CPA exam. Throughout that journey, one question nagged at me: whether in university textbooks or in the firm's training programs and CPA prep courses, the approach was almost always rote — here is the standard, here is the journal entry, here is how you calculate the amount — yet hardly anyone ever explained *why*. Why is each standard written the way it is? What debates and trade-offs did it go through? Are there other possibilities? These questions were never addressed in the classroom and never tested on the exam. Many people who study accounting or sit for professional certifications have never worked at a corporation or an investment firm; lacking practical experience, they find it difficult to stand in the shoes of a financial-statement user and appreciate why a standard was designed a certain way. Take the fair-value measurement of financial instruments, for example: why is it that when market prices fluctuate, some changes are recognized in current-period profit or loss while others are routed to other comprehensive income? The reasoning behind that trade-off is not just a case of "book learning can only take you so far" — the textbooks and training materials simply never discuss it. As a result, accounting becomes something lifeless — a set of rules to be memorized by rote, rather than a living language.

Yet accounting is, by nature, the language of business. And as a language, it inevitably carries the weight of competing interests across business society — especially in the capital markets. These contests, however, are hard for outsiders to see, and most people don't care to look. I remember stumbling upon a news story about the G20 summit discussing the reform of financial instruments standards, and being quite taken aback: how could an accounting standard rise to the agenda of world leaders? I came to understand, gradually, that standards have never been a purely technical matter — every revision mirrors some real-world predicament and reflects the push and pull of competing forces.

The original intent of this series was to bring those behind-the-scenes stories to light, while also offering some cross-standard comparative observations and discussing practical issues that arise in the application of Chinese accounting standards today. A large portion of the articles deals with U.S. standards, for two reasons. First, the United States is a civil society where public policy formation is typically accompanied by robust debate and interest-group bargaining; consequently, the standard-setting process unfolds in the open, leaving a rich trove of historical material to be mined. Second, as one of the world's most developed economies, the United States has long exerted a profound influence on the evolution of accounting standards globally — in fact, international standards and U.S. standards have been in a continuous process of convergence. That is why the stage for these stories is so often set in the American business world. China, in this context, tends to reveal its "Chinese characteristics," making it better suited to a role in comparative study and practical discussion.

To tell each story with some depth, every article ended up on the longer side, requiring roughly fifteen to twenty minutes to read. And although I wrote them as stories, the subject matter is ultimately serious, so reading them does call for a bit of patience.

Writing this series was purely a personal passion project. In publishing it, I offer it simply as a reference — and look forward to more kindred readers discovering it.
