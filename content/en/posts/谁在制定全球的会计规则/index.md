*The Story of Accounting Standards (Part 11)*

Open the annual report of any major global public company — Apple, Toyota, TSMC, Tencent — and behind every number you see is a rule telling the company how to record it. When is revenue considered recognized? Should R&D spending be recorded as an asset or an expense? Does a lease count as a liability? Can goodwill impairment be reversed? These questions may look like technical details, but the answer to each one directly affects the profit figures investors see, which in turn affects stock prices, M&A valuations, loan terms — the way tens of trillions of dollars in global assets are valued.

So here is a very basic question: **who actually writes these rules?**

Accounting is an extremely ancient craft. At the end of the fifteenth century, Luca Pacioli, an Italian Franciscan friar, published a mathematics textbook in Venice that included a chapter systematically describing the double-entry bookkeeping method used by Venetian merchants. This book became the universally acknowledged starting point of accounting history, and Pacioli himself is known as the "**Father of Accounting**" — though strictly speaking, he did not invent double-entry bookkeeping. He was simply the first person to write down the complete method, print it, and pass it on to posterity. Nearly two hundred years before him, merchants in Florence, Genoa, and Venice were already using similar methods to manage their ledgers.

For **five hundred years** after Pacioli, accounting rules evolved mainly through commercial practice: customs among merchants, recommendations from trade associations, demands from bankers, and court precedents gradually accumulated into a set of bookkeeping methods that everyone tacitly followed. **Nobody sat down specifically to "legislate" how accounting should be done.** The idea of a centralized body actually "setting" accounting rules is a twentieth-century development — one shaped by very particular historical circumstances.

The accounting rules used by most countries in the world today — including China — ultimately trace back to **two small organizations**: one in an unremarkable town in Connecticut, called FASB (the Financial Accounting Standards Board); the other in London, called IASB (the International Accounting Standards Board). Together they have only about twenty-odd full-time board members. And the rules decided by these twenty-some people directly affect financial reporting in over one hundred and forty countries and territories worldwide.

A rule system on which the entire global capital market depends is actually set by two private organizations — not governments, not international treaty bodies, not any entity with formal legislative authority. Strictly speaking, the documents they publish carry no legal force in themselves: when FASB issues a standard, legally it is just an internal publication of a private nonprofit organization. But once recognized through formal documents by national regulators — the U.S. SEC, EU regulatory authorities, China's Ministry of Finance — that document immediately acquires the binding force of law. It is a rather peculiar arrangement: **legislative authority is outsourced to expert bodies, while enforcement authority remains with the government.**

The previous articles in this series told the stories behind specific standards — goodwill, fair value, stock options, leases, R&D, revenue, bad debt, consolidated statements. But behind all of those stories lies the same question: **who exactly makes those rules, where, and how?** This article aims to answer that question — to explain how these two organizations came into being, how they operate, and where China fits into this system.

## The American Story: From Industry Self-Governance to an Independent Body

The story of American accounting standards begins with **the stock market crash of 1929**. In the wake of the crash, the United States learned its lesson the hard way, and a few years later established the Securities and Exchange Commission — the SEC — specifically to regulate capital markets. Congress gave the SEC a near-plenary mandate: the authority to set uniform financial reporting rules for all public companies.

Under this mandate, the SEC could have written the standards itself. But the SEC was newly established at the time, short on resources, and accounting technicalities were highly specialized. The SEC's early leadership made a strategic judgment — **rather than having government officials write those technical rules, it was better to hand the work to professionals while the government retained ultimate oversight.** This choice may look like a technical matter, but it defined the basic model of American standard-setting for the next ninety years: **the private sector writes the rules, the government endorses them.**

The entity that took on this task was the American accounting profession's industry association. Starting in the late 1930s, it set up a series of specialized committees to develop standards. But these committees had a structural problem: **the members were all part-time volunteers.** During the day they had full-time jobs at their own firms or companies; they only gathered evenings and weekends to write rules. This part-time structure created two problems. One was slow output that could not keep up with economic change. The other was the more awkward issue of conflicts of interest — committee members found it hard not to think about their own clients' interests when deliberating on rules.

This model limped along for over thirty years. Throughout the 1960s, problems were slowly building: the United States was in the throes of a massive conglomerate merger wave, companies were lobbying harder and harder on accounting treatment to make their financials look better, and every important opinion the standards committee published was met with fierce opposition from industry. Several opinions were derisively called "products of industry lobbying rather than technical analysis." The real tipping point came in 1970. The committee was grappling with a particularly thorny issue: **how to account for business combinations and how to handle goodwill** — an old problem that had been debated since the 1950s without resolution. The committee had originally intended to issue a single comprehensive opinion, but at the voting stage ran into trouble: the members were too deeply divided, and if combined into one document, too many would cast a grudging "agree with reservations" vote, making the whole opinion look like it had barely scraped through and raising questions about its authority. The solution was to **split what should have been one rule into two** — one on the choice of combination method, one on goodwill treatment — so that members could clearly oppose one while fully supporting the other. Even after the split, one of them was published with **6 dissenting votes out of 18 members, landing exactly on the minimum two-thirds majority required for passage.** Shortly after, three of the major accounting firms sent a joint letter to the industry association in November 1970, **publicly declaring they had lost confidence in the standard-setting committee.** The SEC also began signaling: if the profession did not fix this itself, the SEC would step in directly.

In early 1971, the industry association urgently convened meetings to discuss reform and asked a former SEC commissioner to lead the study. This commissioner had been both an SEC regulator and a partner at a major law firm, with insight into how both government and the private sector actually work. He led a small group that gathered input through public hearings and extensive interviews, delivering a report in March 1972. The report's recommendations sounded quite radical at the time: **abolish the old part-time committee and establish an entirely new, independent, full-time standard-setting body.** Board members of the new body would have to work full-time for a salary, sever all employment ties with their former firms and companies, be completely separated from the accounting profession's association, and be **appointed and overseen by an independent foundation.** Any rule published would have to go through due process — public comment, open discussion, publicly recorded votes.

The report was adopted in full. In July 1973, FASB officially began operations. By the end of that year, the SEC issued a brief administrative release formally stating: **FASB's standards would be recognized by the SEC as authoritative, and U.S. public companies should comply with them.** That administrative release remains the basis of FASB's legitimacy to this day — no law directly grants FASB power; rather, the SEC used its own administrative authority to "recognize" FASB's rules.

But this model had a long-term vulnerability: **although the foundation was structurally independent of the accounting profession's association, its money was not.** The early arrangement had FASB's parent foundation solicit voluntary contributions from large corporations and major accounting firms — meaning the donors were precisely the institutions directly affected by FASB's standards. The independence issues created by this structure surfaced intermittently over decades. When FASB moved to reform stock option accounting in 1996, some large corporations even tried to counter FASB by exerting control over its parent foundation, and the SEC chairman had to publicly threaten to revoke the 1973 release to stabilize the situation. The problem was not fully resolved until 2002 — the year the Enron and WorldCom accounting scandals shocked the nation and Congress passed a major overhaul of capital market regulation. The law included a provision specifically addressing FASB's funding: **from then on, FASB's expenses would no longer depend on voluntary corporate contributions but would be funded by a mandatory "accounting support fee" levied by the SEC on all public companies,** with each company paying a share proportional to its market capitalization. FASB and its parent foundation also pledged to stop accepting any contributions from the accounting industry. With this, FASB was freed from dependence on regulated entities at both the governance and funding levels.

From the 1929 crash to the 2002 law, it took the United States a full seventy years to gradually refine the peculiar arrangement of "a private body setting public rules" into a relatively stable system.

## The International Story: From Seeking the Lowest Common Denominator to Global Standard-Setter

Interestingly, in the same year FASB was born in 1973, something also happened in London.

This was not entirely a coincidence. Accounting profession associations from nine countries and territories — including the U.S., U.K., Canada, Australia, France, Germany, and Japan — jointly established an international accounting standards committee in London. **The American accounting profession's association was both a driving force behind the domestic establishment of FASB and a founding member of this new London organization.**

This London organization was not the product of an international treaty, not a United Nations agency, not any intergovernmental body. It was merely a voluntary federation of accounting profession associations from nine countries — something like "an international association of industry associations." The standards it issued had no legal force in any country; whether each nation adopted them was entirely its own affair.

For the first decade or so, the organization's ambitions were very modest. Board members were part-time, meeting a few times a year to discuss technical issues. Its early goal was not to unify national standards but to find the **lowest common denominator** among existing practices across countries — accounting historians describe this phase as "**coordination through compromise**": there was goodwill, but nobody was really willing to give up the rules their own country had grown accustomed to. The advisory standards it published were mainly adopted in developing countries that lacked independent standard-setting systems; in major countries like the U.S., U.K., Germany, and Japan, its influence on domestic standards was negligible.

What truly transformed it from a coordinating body into the world's de facto legislator was a key institution that few people mention today — **the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO).** This organization is a federation of national securities regulators, a kind of "United Nations of securities commissions," with its secretariat in Madrid (the organization still exists today, with over two hundred members covering more than ninety-five percent of global securities markets; the China Securities Regulatory Commission is a full member). In the early 1990s, IOSCO recognized a practical problem: cross-border listings were growing, but the lack of uniform standards across countries was making regulatory work extremely difficult. IOSCO itself had no capacity to write a complete set of accounting standards, so it **chose to partner with the London organization**: London would develop a comprehensive set of "core standards," and if they met the bar, IOSCO would recommend that national regulators accept financial statements from cross-border listed companies prepared under these standards. This agreement, signed in 1995, was the turning point — it gave the London organization, which had previously had no real "clients," a clear mission and a genuine counterpart for the first time. In 1999, the core standards were completed on schedule.

Meanwhile, the U.S. SEC also published a lengthy document in early 2000 raising 26 questions that challenged the quality and governance structure of these core standards point by point. The subtext was clear: if you want to become a standard-setter recognized by the United States, you must further transform yourselves — you need full-time board members, stable funding, due process, and independent governance.

The London organization accepted the challenge. In fact, discussions about restructuring had long been underway internally. In April 2001, the transformation was complete — the original part-time committee was replaced by a full-time body called the IASB, still headquartered in London. The new body was modeled entirely on FASB's design: full-time board members, severed ties with former employers, appointment and oversight by an independent foundation, and clear due process. Its standards were also renamed "International Financial Reporting Standards" — the IFRS that everyone knows today.

But the newborn IASB had a fundamental legitimacy problem. FASB had the SEC's administrative release as endorsement, which gave it binding force over U.S. public companies. The IASB **had no direct endorsement from any country's regulator.** It was merely a private nonprofit operating in London; any country could simply ignore it. The only way for it to become a "global standard-setter" was to get countries to choose to recognize it on their own.

Right around the time IASB completed its restructuring, the European Union was making a major strategic decision. As early as the Lisbon European Council in March 2000, EU leaders had set a goal: **to build a single European financial market by 2005.** This goal required a uniform set of financial reporting rules as a prerequisite. The EU had only two choices: use American standards or use this new set of international standards. Choosing American standards would mean handing European companies' financial disclosures entirely over to Washington; choosing international standards would allow the EU to retain a degree of influence through this new London body. In July 2002, after two years of discussion and public consultation, the EU formally adopted a regulation: **starting in 2005, all companies listed in the EU would be required to prepare consolidated financial statements using IFRS.** This single regulation instantly transformed IFRS from a set of advisory texts into rules with binding force over approximately seven thousand European listed companies. Within barely a year, the IASB went from a freshly restructured small organization to Europe's de facto accounting regulator.

This decision triggered an enormous chain reaction. Once seven thousand major European companies were all using IFRS, other countries had powerful incentives to follow — if you wanted your domestic companies to raise capital easily in Europe, and wanted cross-border investors to be able to compare your companies' data with their European peers, you had to move toward IFRS. From 2005 to today, the number of countries adopting IFRS has snowballed: Australia, South Africa, Hong Kong, South Korea, Russia, Brazil, Canada... Today, over one hundred and forty countries and territories require or permit the use of IFRS.

Only the United States and a handful of other countries still maintain their own standard systems. It is worth discussing specifically: **why didn't the U.S. follow?** So many other countries switched, yet the U.S. did not. The reasons are multifaceted. FASB had been operating for decades, and U.S. tax law, contract law, and various regulatory rules were deeply intertwined with American domestic standards; switching systems would be extremely costly — that is the first reason. The U.S. litigation environment differs significantly from other countries — American standards tend to provide explicit rules to facilitate courtroom defense, while international standards lean more toward a "principles-based" approach, a fundamental difference in legal culture — that is the second. Third is the independence concern — the SEC has always had questions about IASB's governance structure and funding sources, and was unwilling to hand U.S. public companies' financial disclosure standards over to a London body that the U.S. could not directly regulate. Fourth is market weight — **the sheer scale of the U.S. capital market gives the United States the leverage to insist on its own rules.** Most companies worldwide, whether they want to or not, need to come to the U.S. to raise capital, issue debt, and complete major acquisitions. This asymmetry allows the U.S. to say "I'll use my own rules; you adapt," while most other countries lack that leverage. Between 2008 and 2012, the SEC did seriously consider full adoption of IFRS and even published a "roadmap," but the 2008 financial crisis significantly drained the political momentum for convergence, and the effort ultimately fizzled. What the U.S. chose instead was "convergence without adoption" — even without formally using IFRS, U.S. standards have been continuously "converging" with IFRS over the past twenty years: the two bodies signed an agreement as early as 2002 committing to eliminate differences, and major standards from the past decade on leases, revenue, and financial instruments were jointly developed and simultaneously issued, with content essentially identical on both sides.

A final note on where IASB's money comes from. Unlike FASB, the IASB has no mandatory collection mechanism backed by any country. Its funding comes mainly from several sources: voluntary contributions from national governments, corporate donations, and revenue from selling the copyright and publications of its standards. The largest country contributors include Canada, Germany, Japan, **and China** — according to publicly disclosed data, China contributed approximately £4.13 million to the IFRS Foundation in 2023, tied for second with Germany, behind only Canada. That same year, the IFRS Foundation also opened an ISSB office in Beijing, supported under a memorandum of understanding signed between China's Ministry of Finance and the Foundation. This makes Beijing one of six permanent offices of the IFRS Foundation worldwide (the other five are London, Frankfurt, Montreal, San Francisco, and Tokyo).

But there is a long-standing awkwardness here: **of the one hundred and forty-plus countries and territories using IFRS, only about a quarter actually contribute money to the IASB** — the rest are essentially using the rules for free. The IASB's parent foundation has been pushing a "fair share" funding model for years, but progress has been slow. A more immediate signal: in 2024, the IFRS Foundation reported an operating deficit and launched an internal "cost reduction and transformation plan." From a funding structure perspective, this organization remains far more fragile than FASB to this day.

## How Are Standards Actually Made?

The day-to-day operations of these two organizations are unfamiliar territory for many investors. This is actually a shame, because the process is **far more open than most people imagine** — you can even watch their meetings live online.

Their workflow goes roughly as follows. A topic must first make it onto the agenda. It could come from concerns raised by regulators, problems flagged by investors, loopholes exposed by major scandals, or deficiencies the standard-setters themselves discover during post-implementation reviews. Every few years, both organizations publicly solicit input to determine their work priorities going forward.

Once a topic is placed on the agenda, it enters a research phase, which typically produces a "discussion paper" that lays out the issues and solicits public comment. A discussion paper is not a formal draft standard — it is more like a questionnaire asking, "We're thinking about these issues; what do you think?" Anyone — companies, investors, academics, regulators, even ordinary citizens — can submit written comments. All of these comments are published in full on the organization's website.

Based on the feedback received on the discussion paper, the organization drafts an "exposure draft," which is the first formal draft of the standard. The exposure draft typically has a comment period of several months, during which feedback is again collected from all parties. The organization also holds roundtable discussions, working group meetings, and one-on-one interviews to gather input as broadly as possible.

Then comes the redeliberation phase. Board members discuss each key decision in the draft point by point **in public meetings,** based on the feedback received. These meetings are entirely open to the public — you can find live webcasts on the websites of both organizations, and recordings and meeting minutes are available afterward. Each board member's position and every vote result are recorded. The final standard text is accompanied by a document called "Basis for Conclusions," which explains in detail why particular decisions were made, what alternatives were considered, and why certain objections were rejected. Dissenting board members can publish their dissenting opinions, which are included in full.

This process sounds lengthy, and it genuinely is. A major standard typically takes five to ten years from the time it is placed on the agenda to final publication. The revenue recognition standard discussed earlier took twelve years; the lease standard took roughly fifteen. This slowness is sometimes criticized as sluggish, but it is also the key to the system's legitimacy — **because it exposes "how the rules came to be" fully to the light of day.**

If you want to truly understand the considerations behind standard-setting, the best approach is to read the **Basis for Conclusions and comment letter archives.** On the websites of both organizations, every standard has a complete historical record — from the initial discussion paper to final publication, with all public comments available for free download. In these documents, you will see how a rule was pulled in every direction before it was finalized: which major companies strongly opposed it, which investor organizations strongly supported it, which academics raised technical objections, and how the board members weighed all that feedback.

## China's Position

At this point, a natural question arises: where does China stand in this global system?

Starting domestically: China's enterprise accounting standards are set directly by the **Ministry of Finance.** Under the Ministry, a dedicated department handles accounting affairs, and below that sits an Accounting Standards Committee that serves as an advisory body — but this committee has no independent decision-making authority; it is merely the Ministry's technical support unit. The final standard texts are issued as Ministry of Finance documents, with legal force derived from the Accounting Law and the Ministry's administrative authority as a constituent department of the State Council.

This structure is completely different from the American model of "private body plus government endorsement." In China, the legislator is the government itself, and the chain of authorization is very clear. But this clarity has another side: **in China, accounting standards are not merely a technical tool; they also serve as a policy instrument.** Behind some specific design choices in the standards, alongside technical judgments, there are also explicit policy considerations.

China's large-scale convergence with international standards truly began at the start of the new century. In November 2005, China's Accounting Standards Committee and the IASB issued a joint statement in Beijing, confirming that China would promote "**substantial convergence**" between its enterprise accounting standards and IFRS. The backdrop was that after China's accession to the WTO, more and more enterprises were pursuing overseas listings and cross-border financing, making international comparability of financial reports a hard requirement. In 2006, the Ministry of Finance issued a complete set of new enterprise accounting standards, which became mandatory for A-share listed companies in 2007 and were subsequently extended to large state-owned enterprises and medium-sized companies over the following years. This new set of standards was consistent with IFRS of the time on most provisions.

In 2010, the Ministry of Finance issued another document clarifying an important distinction: **"convergence" does not mean "direct adoption."** Some other countries and territories internationally took the "direct adoption" route — transplanting IFRS wholesale. Hong Kong's financial reporting standards (HKFRS), for instance, are essentially IFRS with a name change. China chose the "convergence" route — **maintaining consistency with IFRS in principles and substance while retaining the flexibility to make adjustments based on domestic circumstances in specific provisions.** The document also used the phrase "continuing convergence" — meaning convergence is not a one-time destination but an ongoing process. Whenever IFRS is revised, China reassesses whether and how to follow suit.

What results has this strategy produced in practice? Two concrete examples illustrate the point.

The first example is the rule that **impairment of long-lived assets may not be reversed.** International standards allow reversal of previously recognized asset impairment under certain conditions — if an asset's value recovers after an impairment has been recognized, the prior impairment charge can be written back. But Chinese standards explicitly stipulate: for fixed assets, intangible assets, long-term equity investments, and goodwill, **once an impairment is recognized, it may not be reversed** (this rule does not apply to inventories and financial assets). This rule has no counterpart in international standards. China's insistence on keeping it is not because its technical judgment is superior to the international approach, but because the A-share market historically saw widespread cases of using impairment reversals for earnings management — some companies would take massive impairment charges in poor-performance years to "take a bath," then reverse them the following year to manufacture accounting profits. The Ministry of Finance chose a blanket approach to plug this loophole entirely. This is a textbook case of "policy considerations overriding technical uniformity."

The second example runs in the opposite direction — a case where **China influenced international standards.** The earliest international related-party disclosure rules required that all "entities under common control" disclose their inter-company transactions. For China, this was a nearly unworkable requirement, because Chinese state-owned enterprises are all under the common control of SASAC and various levels of government. If all of them were treated as related parties, the related-party transaction disclosures in a single large SOE's annual report would run to dozens of pages. After 2005, China's Ministry of Finance continuously raised this issue through regular dialogue with the IASB. **In November 2009, the IASB issued a revised related-party standard that specifically added a partial disclosure exemption for "government-related entities"** — an exemption that was written almost exactly to China's specifications. This demonstrates that even in a system that appears to have been designed entirely by developed countries, a large emerging-market country that consistently makes its voice heard can genuinely change the content of the rules.

In fact, China's accounting standards today are already highly consistent with international standards in substance. The former chairman of the IASB publicly stated that "Chinese standards have achieved equivalence with international standards." One intuitive piece of evidence is the annual reports of A+H dual-listed companies: ICBC, Bank of China, CICC, and other institutions explicitly disclosed in their 2024 annual reports that net profit attributable to shareholders and net assets prepared under Chinese enterprise accounting standards and under IFRS showed "no difference." The most notable remaining difference is probably the aforementioned rule barring the reversal of long-lived asset impairment. For cross-border investors, this means the accounting "translation cost" between China and international standards is already very low.

At the international level, China's participation is far more comprehensive than many people realize, operating through several different channels. The most formal channel is **placing representatives in the IASB's various bodies** — for nearly twenty years, there has consistently been a full-time board member from mainland China participating in the entire standard-setting process at the IASB; the IFRS Foundation's Trustees and the Standards Advisory Council have also consistently included Chinese seats; and China's Ministry of Finance is a member of the IFRS Foundation's Monitoring Board, alongside the U.S. SEC, the European Commission, and Japan's Financial Services Agency. The second channel is **written comment letters**: China's Ministry of Finance, the China Securities Regulatory Commission, and several large accounting firms have consistently submitted written feedback during every IFRS comment period — if you check the comment archive for any major standard on the IFRS Foundation's website, you will find comments from various Chinese institutions. The third channel is **regular dialogue mechanisms**: the Ministry of Finance and the IASB hold two scheduled meetings per year, which were elevated to high-level convergence talks after 2010, specifically addressing the direction of international standard reform and specific issues of concern to China. The fourth channel is **regional organizations**: in 2009, China led the establishment of the "Asian-Oceanian Standard-Setters Group," organizing emerging-market economies in the Asia-Pacific region to speak collectively in the IFRS development process.

One recent event particularly illustrates China's posture within this system. After 2021, momentum built internationally for sustainability disclosure standards. The IFRS Foundation established a new body specifically for this purpose — the International Sustainability Standards Board (covered in a previous article in this series), a sister body to the IASB. The standards issued by this new body adopted a "**single financial materiality**" perspective — focusing only on how sustainability issues affect a company's finances. But the Sustainability Reporting Guidelines published in 2024 by the Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Beijing stock exchanges chose a different path: **requiring disclosure of both the impact of sustainability issues on the company's finances and the company's own external impact on the economy, society, and the environment.** This aligns with the EU's approach but diverges markedly from the path taken by IASB's sister body.

The situation in the United States represents yet a third approach. In March 2024, the SEC issued a climate disclosure rule that also adopted the "single financial materiality" perspective. But the rule was immediately challenged in court by multiple state governments and industry groups and has been judicially stayed. **After the Trump administration took office in 2025, as part of its anti-ESG regulatory policy, the SEC announced it would no longer defend this rule** — the official reasons cited were excessive compliance costs and that the SEC may lack the statutory authority to require climate disclosures from companies. The rule has effectively been rendered void. In other words, on the emerging issue of sustainability disclosure, the world has roughly split into **three camps**: the "single financial materiality" approach of the IASB's sister body and the original SEC position; the "double materiality" approach championed by the EU and China; and the United States' current de facto "withdrawal from mandatory disclosure."

China's choice to follow the EU's path rather than the IASB sister body's path here is not a minor technical adjustment but a directional statement. Behind this choice lies a clear logic. In traditional financial accounting, China is willing to bear the costs of convergence — because international capital market comparability is a hard requirement; without it, Chinese companies cannot raise capital overseas, and foreign investors cannot understand A-share companies. But on issues like sustainability disclosure, the situation is different: these are deeply intertwined with national policy and deeply intertwined with societal value choices, and they are difficult to cover with a single "neutral technical framework" designed by a handful of developed countries. **On which areas are worth converging and which areas call for independent judgment, China has its own set of criteria for making those trade-offs.**

## A System Still Evolving

At this point, the whole story might look like a settled system: the U.S. has FASB, the world has IASB, China maintains continuing convergence with international standards — everyone in their place. But in reality, the challenges these two organizations face today are no less daunting than what that part-time committee confronted in 1970.

The thorniest issue is **the digital economy and intangible assets.** Existing standards grew out of a world of factories, inventories, and tangible assets. Data, algorithms, platforms, user networks, and brand influence — the things worth the most today — have almost no place in accounting standards. For a leading software company, the assets on its balance sheet often represent only a fraction of its true value. Should standards address this gap? How? This is a major unresolved question.

The second issue is **how to integrate sustainability disclosure with financial reporting.** As discussed, the paths among the EU, China, and the U.S. in this area have already visibly diverged. Whether these two types of information can be clearly presented within a single set of financial statements in the future, or whether they will remain two separate systems forever, is a question nobody can answer today.

The third issue is **the pull of geopolitics.** The expansion of IFRS over the past twenty years coincided with the rising tide of globalization — everyone believed that "uniform rules are better than fragmented ones." That premise is no longer as solid today. Changes in U.S.-China relations, countries reasserting local regulatory sovereignty, and the politicization of cross-border capital flows — every one of these is eroding the assumption of a "globally unified accounting language."

Perhaps twenty years from now, looking back, we will realize that today is yet another inflection point for the accounting standard-setting profession — just like the crisis of the early 1970s. Back then, a group of part-time committee members were bitterly divided over the business combinations issue, three major firms published an open letter declaring a loss of confidence, and nobody realized they were witnessing the end of one era and the beginning of another.
