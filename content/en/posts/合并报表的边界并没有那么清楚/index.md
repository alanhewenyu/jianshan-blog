*Stories of Accounting Standards (Part 10)*

## Control and Consolidation

Consolidated financial statements combine the assets, liabilities, revenues, and profits of a parent company and its controlled subsidiaries onto a single set of statements, giving outside investors a complete picture of "the entire group." The starting point for consolidation is therefore a core question: who should be included? The critical judgment is about control.

Under the current IFRS 10 and the converged Chinese Accounting Standard No. 33, an investor has "control" over an investee when three conditions are simultaneously met: first, the investor has "power" over the investee — the ability to direct its relevant activities; second, the investor has exposure or rights to "variable returns" from its involvement with the investee — meaning the returns fluctuate with the investee's performance; and third, the investor has the ability to use that power to affect the amount of those variable returns. These three elements are known as the "three elements of control," and all must be present. Whenever control exists, the investee must be included in the investor's consolidated financial statements.

But this seemingly clear-cut rule actually leaves companies considerable room for judgment in practice. Each of the three elements involves "substance-based judgment" rather than "mechanical calculation" — what counts as "the ability to direct relevant activities"? What counts as "variable returns"? There are no numerical thresholds to apply; accountants, auditors, and management must exercise professional judgment in each specific situation. The same equity structure and the same contractual arrangements might lead to different conclusions at different companies. This is precisely what makes consolidation so difficult — its core concept is "substance over form," but "substance" itself is an open-ended matter of judgment.

Once the determination to consolidate has been made, standards must answer another question: when one company gains control over another, at what value should the acquired entity's assets and liabilities enter the acquirer's statements?

In general, a business combination is treated as one party "purchasing" the other, and should be accounted for using the "acquisition method": the acquirer measures the acquiree's assets and liabilities at fair value, and recognizes the difference between the consideration paid and the fair value of identifiable net assets as goodwill; comparative period data is not restated retrospectively.

But the standards also provide an exception. If the two companies participating in the combination are under the same ultimate control both before and after the transaction, and that control is not transitory, the combination is classified as a "business combination under common control." The accounting logic is that the combination has not fundamentally changed the group's overall control relationships — it is merely an internal rearrangement of assets within the group. Therefore, the combination is recorded at carrying values, no new goodwill is created, and comparative financial data may be restated retrospectively. This method is called the "pooling-of-interests method," and the next section is dedicated to it — it has quite a story in international accounting standards.

Looking at the two categories of combination methods side by side, you can sense where the real difficulty of consolidation standards lies: determining "whether control exists" is already hard, but determining "whether common control exists" is even harder. The former requires drawing a line between substance and form; the latter requires drawing another line between the abstract "ultimate controlling party" and the concrete "group boundary."

## The Pooling-of-Interests Method

The story of the pooling-of-interests method begins in the United States in 1970. In August of that year, the Accounting Principles Board (APB, the predecessor of FASB) issued Opinion No. 16, *Business Combinations*, which formally established that both the purchase method and the pooling-of-interests method were legitimate accounting methods for business combinations. However, companies were not free to choose between them — a business combination had to be classified based on the economic substance of the transaction: only a "pooling of interests" meeting a strict set of conditions could use the pooling-of-interests method; those that did not had to use the purchase method.

APB 16 laid out 12 specific criteria, covering the attributes of the combining parties, the manner of the transaction, and post-combination arrangements. A few examples: the combining parties could not have been subsidiaries of each other prior to the combination; the combination had to be achieved through a stock-for-stock exchange without significant cash or other consideration; original shareholders' voting rights could not be restricted; significant assets of the acquired party could not be disposed of within two years after the combination; and so on. All 12 had to be met to use the pooling-of-interests method; failure to meet any single one meant the purchase method was required.

The difference in financial statement impact between the two methods was enormous. For the same combination, using the pooling-of-interests method often resulted in dramatically higher reported profits for the combined company compared to the purchase method. There were two main reasons. First, under the purchase method, assets were recorded at fair value, which was typically higher than book value, resulting in higher depreciation and amortization that consumed profits each year. Second, under the U.S. accounting standards of the time — APB Opinion No. 17 (issued concurrently with APB 16) — goodwill itself also had to be amortized over a maximum of 40 years, consuming profits each year as well. In other words, a combination under the purchase method would drag down the acquirer's profits for decades into the future, while the pooling-of-interests method, because it produced no goodwill and made no fair value adjustments, carried none of this burden.

One can imagine how desperately companies wanted to use the pooling-of-interests method. During the M&A boom of the 1990s, stock-for-stock mergers between public companies were frequently structured specifically to meet APB 16's 12 criteria — some transactions even cost hundreds of millions of additional dollars solely to ensure they could qualify for pooling-of-interests treatment. A frequently cited example is AT&T's 1991 acquisition of NCR: in the later stages of negotiations, AT&T raised the exchange ratio specifically to persuade NCR shareholders to accept a pure stock deal — because if NCR shareholders refused and cash consideration had to be mixed in, the entire combination would fail to meet the pooling-of-interests "stock-for-stock" requirement. The extra payment here was in stock rather than cash, so it did not violate APB 16's restriction against "significant cash or other consideration." By some estimates, AT&T paid at least $50 million extra — and possibly as much as $500 million — essentially as the price of admission for pooling-of-interests qualification.

FASB took note of this preference. In June 2001, FASB issued SFAS 141 and SFAS 142 — two companion standards that accomplished two things in tandem: first, formally abolishing the pooling-of-interests method and requiring all business combinations to use the purchase method; and second, simultaneously eliminating mandatory goodwill amortization in favor of annual impairment testing. Abolishing the pooling-of-interests method was meant to close the loophole of "using the choice of combination method to beautify profits"; eliminating goodwill amortization was meant to ease the pushback against the purchase method's "40 years of profit erosion" — this latter step was discussed in detail in the first goodwill article of this series.

Three years later, the IASB followed suit. In IFRS 3, issued in 2004, the pooling-of-interests method was explicitly excluded from International Financial Reporting Standards.

When China issued its new Accounting Standards for Business Enterprises in 2006, it made a different choice.

*Accounting Standard for Business Enterprises No. 20 — Business Combinations* did not fully abolish the pooling-of-interests method. Instead, it retained the method specifically for "business combinations under common control." The practical considerations behind this distinction were very concrete: state-owned enterprise (SOE) reform was then driving asset consolidation within groups, and a large volume of asset transfers was occurring between different subsidiaries under the same ultimate controlling party. If the purchase method were applied uniformly, every internal asset transfer within a group would require fresh fair value assessments, generating large amounts of "internal goodwill" — which would neither reflect economic substance nor be desirable for the complexity it would add to SOE financial statements. China's solution: where the same ultimate controlling party's control remains unchanged, intra-group combinations are recorded at carrying values, no goodwill is generated, and comparative periods may be restated retrospectively. In Chinese standards, this method is typically called the "carrying value method" — it is essentially the same method as the pooling-of-interests method from the American story, just under a different name.

This retention created a small but significant divergence between Chinese and international accounting standards.

For the IASB, this divergence was a "reduce diversity" to-do item. One of the IASB's core objectives is to ensure that listed companies worldwide applying IFRS use the same accounting treatment for the same type of transaction, so that cross-border investors can directly compare financial statements across countries. Business combinations under common control are explicitly excluded from the scope of IFRS 3 — meaning IFRS has no unified rule for such transactions, and companies applying IFRS in various jurisdictions develop their own accounting policies under IAS 8 (Changes in Accounting Policies). The result: some companies record at carrying values, others at fair values, and the reported outcomes for the same type of transaction vary enormously. The IASB wanted to bring this under control.

In 2007, the IASB added "business combinations under common control" as a research project. In November 2020, the IASB published a Discussion Paper on *Business Combinations under Common Control*, proposing a compromise: if the receiving entity has external minority shareholders beyond the controlling shareholder, the acquisition method should apply; in other cases, the carrying value method could be used. The logic was that once the acquirer has external minority shareholders, the acquired assets are "new" to those external shareholders, and recording at fair value better reflects their true interests; but if the acquirer is a wholly owned subsidiary with no external minority shareholders, the combination is purely an internal transfer, and carrying values are more appropriate.

In theory, this proposal could both reduce cross-jurisdictional divergence and respect the economic substance of transactions. But the 2021 comment period revealed a stark divide. The IASB's own project summary was quite direct: nearly all comments from China supported applying the carrying value method to all business combinations under common control; nearly all comments from other jurisdictions supported the acquisition method.

The two camps had different concerns. Those supporting the acquisition method worried about "accounting arbitrage" — if the carrying value method were broadly permitted, groups could transfer assets at carrying values into listed subsidiaries through internal restructuring, bypassing fair value reassessment and preventing external investors from seeing the true transaction price. Those supporting the carrying value method — primarily China — worried about "reflecting economic substance": intra-group asset transfers do not generate new economic benefits when viewed from the group level, and insisting on fair value recognition with resulting goodwill is an artificially manufactured accounting outcome. Both concerns have merit. The underlying disagreement is really between two different ways of thinking.

In November 2023, the IASB met to decide the project's direction. The board first voted on whether to continue exploring recognition and measurement requirements; the conclusion was not to continue. Then, on the remaining two options — developing only disclosure requirements or terminating the entire project — the IASB members present voted unanimously for the latter: termination. The rationale: given the deep divergence of views, the cost of developing new rules would likely exceed the improvements to financial reporting they would bring. In April 2024, the IASB published the project summary, formally bringing the entire research project to a close.

The IASB explicitly stated in its project summary: since IFRS has no dedicated rules for business combinations under common control, companies applying IFRS in various jurisdictions will continue to develop their own accounting policies under IAS 8's hierarchy. In other words, multiple approaches in this area will continue to coexist legitimately — China's carrying value method and other jurisdictions' acquisition method will both persist.

## Consolidated Financial Statements in the Chinese Context

The boundary question in consolidated financial statements manifests in several distinctive ways in the Chinese context. Some arise from circumvention of the consolidation scope, some from accounting method issues created by the retention of the pooling-of-interests method, and some from accounting rules being used as tools in ways their designers never anticipated. Let us look at three cases.

#### The "Minority Shareholders" of Real Estate Developers

This case is a specific manifestation of the consolidation scope issue. The standards require project companies to be consolidated, with assets and liabilities properly presented, but the true nature of the minority interest line item was distorted.

Between 2017 and 2020, a pervasive pattern appeared among real estate companies listed on the A-share and Hong Kong markets: minority interest in consolidated statements ballooned dramatically, yet the corresponding profit or loss attributable to minority shareholders barely grew.

Take Jinke Properties as an example. From 2017 to 2019, Jinke's minority interest grew from RMB 2.597 billion to RMB 14.586 billion, then to RMB 24.784 billion — nearly a tenfold increase in three years. Yet the profit attributable to minority shareholders over the same period was only RMB 281 million, RMB 191 million, and RMB 760 million respectively — implying a return of only a few percent on their equity, while the net profit attributable to Jinke's own shareholders grew at a far higher rate. The contrast at Zhongliang Holdings was even more striking: its 2020 interim report showed minority interest of RMB 16.57 billion versus equity attributable to the parent of RMB 8.85 billion. At Evergrande Group, minority interest had consistently exceeded parent equity since 2017; in the years before its debt crisis erupted, the cumulative funds absorbed through the minority interest channel reached hundreds of billions of yuan.

The vast majority of these "minority shareholders" were not genuine financial investors but trust companies, private equity funds, bank wealth management products, and similar institutions. They invested in project companies in the form of "equity," but simultaneously signed buyback agreements, shortfall make-up agreements, and performance bet agreements with the listed company and its controlling shareholder — agreeing to exit at predetermined prices at specified times and essentially receiving fixed returns. This arrangement is known in the industry as "equity in name, debt in substance" (*ming gu shi zhai*).

Why did everyone do this? There were two benefits for real estate developers. First, it lowered the consolidated balance sheet's debt-to-asset ratio — trust funds entering project companies as "equity" rather than "debt" dramatically suppressed the listed company's leverage ratios. Second, it circumvented financing regulations — after 2016, regulators increasingly tightened direct financing channels for real estate developers, and project-level "equity investment" became an unrestricted alternative pathway.

Why didn't regulators and auditors put a stop to it sooner? There were two layers to this problem. At the regulatory level, side agreements were by definition undisclosed; regulators could not detect the true situation from consolidated statement figures alone and could only discover them through targeted project inspections. The auditor-level issue was more subtle: side agreements were typically not signed at the listed company level but at the project company level, or even between the controlling shareholder and the trust company, bypassing the listed company's formal document processes. When auditors performed confirmations and inquiries, the controlling shareholder and trust company had a shared incentive to conceal the agreements' existence. Moreover, since equity-in-name-debt-in-substance was virtually an industry norm from 2017 to 2019, the "everyone does it" mentality itself softened auditors' willingness to challenge. It was only when major developers like Evergrande and Sunac successively revealed their debt crises that the true liabilities hidden under this classification became widely recognized by the market.

In August 2020, the pilot program for the "three red lines" for real estate developers began. Two of the three red lines were leverage-related metrics — the debt-to-asset ratio (excluding contract liabilities) must not exceed 70%, and the net debt ratio must not exceed 100%. For developers already dependent on equity-in-name-debt-in-substance, the three red lines actually made the practice more urgent: with on-balance-sheet financing constrained, there was even greater need to keep suppressing on-balance-sheet leverage metrics. But regulators began systematically scrutinizing this channel from that point on. On February 18, 2025, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC) issued the *Rules for State-owned Asset Transactions* (SASAC Document No. 17 of 2025), which in Article 69 explicitly prohibits arrangements such as "equity in name, debt in substance" in SOE capital increase transactions conducted through public exchanges. This was the first time a regulator explicitly named and banned this practice at the SOE transaction rule level.

The boundary of consolidated statements was breached by real estate developers in a remarkably covert way — not by kicking entities out of the consolidation scope, but by disguising liabilities as equity, making the "completeness" of consolidated statements quantitatively intact but qualitatively distorted beyond recognition.

#### Business Combinations Under Common Control

In September 2024, the A-share market saw two enormous, nearly symmetrical absorption mergers launched within days of each other.

On the evening of September 2, China State Shipbuilding Corporation Limited (CSSC, 600150) and China Shipbuilding Industry Company Limited (CSIC, 601989) simultaneously announced trading halts effective the morning of September 3, initiating a major asset restructuring in which CSSC would issue A shares to all CSIC shareholders in a share-swap absorption merger. Three days later, on the evening of September 5, Guotai Junan Securities and Haitong Securities also announced trading halts, initiating an absorption merger of the same nature.

These two transactions shared almost every characteristic: both were unprecedented large-scale absorption mergers in the A-share market, each valued in the hundreds of billions of yuan; both were state-directed consolidations of same-industry competitors aimed at resolving horizontal competition among state-controlled companies; both required approval from relevant State Council departments and had to complete the review process at the CSRC and the stock exchange. Yet their accounting treatments differed.

CSSC's absorption merger of CSIC was classified as a business combination under common control.

The key to this determination was an event in October 2019. That year, the State Council approved the merger of China Shipbuilding Industry Group (commonly known as "South Ship") and China Shipbuilding Heavy Industry Group (commonly known as "North Ship") to form China State Shipbuilding Corporation Group. From that moment on, both listed companies became subsidiaries of the same group parent — China State Shipbuilding Corporation Group. From October 2019 to September 2024, this "within the same enterprise group" status had existed stably for five years, far exceeding the "non-transitory control" threshold required by the standards. On September 5, 2025, CSIC's A shares were formally delisted, and CSSC completed the absorption merger at a total transaction value of RMB 115.15 billion — the largest absorption merger in A-share history — without generating any new goodwill on the consolidated statements.

One detail of this transaction's accounting treatment is worth explaining. China State Shipbuilding Corporation Group and its concert parties held 47.63% of CSIC's equity before the transaction; the remaining 52.37% was dispersed among CSIC's minority shareholders. This meant the 47.63% was already under common control before the merger, while the 52.37% belonged to external third-party shareholders. The CSRC's *Regulatory Rule Application Guidelines — Accounting Category No. 1* provides specific guidance for this type of "business combination under common control where equity is simultaneously acquired from both the controlling shareholder and third parties," treating the controlling interest and the minority interest under two different logics.

In simple terms, CSSC had to bring CSIC's entirety into the consolidation scope at carrying values and retrospectively restate comparative period statements — as if CSSC had held CSIC through the group parent since October 2019 (when China State Shipbuilding Corporation Group was established). In the restated comparative consolidated statements, 100% of CSIC's assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses are combined line by line, with 47.63% attributed to the parent company's shareholders in the equity structure and 52.37% presented as "minority interest" and "profit or loss attributable to minority shareholders."

What is not restated is the "purchase action" for the 52.37% — the difference between the share-swap consideration CSSC paid for the 52.37% minority interest and the corresponding carrying value of net assets (in plain terms, the minority interest premium). On the merger date in 2025, CSSC acquired the 52.37% minority interest in a single share swap. Chinese standards classify this as an "equity transaction": a parent company purchasing minority shares of an already-controlled subsidiary does not constitute a new business combination, does not require remeasurement of assets and liabilities at fair value, and does not generate goodwill. The minority interest premium is recognized as a one-time adjustment to capital reserve (with any shortfall charged against retained earnings).

Guotai Junan's absorption merger of Haitong Securities took a different path.

Guotai Junan's ultimate controller is Shanghai International Group Co., Ltd., which is 100% owned by the Shanghai SASAC. Haitong Securities had a relatively dispersed ownership structure with no controlling shareholder or ultimate controller; its largest shareholder was Shanghai Guosheng Group (holding approximately 10.38%), which is also 100% owned by the Shanghai SASAC. From the perspective of "state-asset supervisory relationships," both companies are financial enterprises supervised by the Shanghai SASAC. But the "within the same enterprise group" standard that accounting standards care about looks at a different layer — Shanghai International Group and Shanghai Guosheng Group have never undergone a top-level consolidation like the 2019 merger of the North and South Ship groups. They are two parallel state-owned capital operating platforms under the Shanghai SASAC. Under the strict "within the same enterprise group" criterion, Guotai Junan and Haitong Securities were not in the same enterprise group.

The accounting determination was therefore clear: a business combination not under common control, accounted for using the acquisition method. The acquirer recognizes the acquiree's identifiable assets and liabilities at fair value, and the difference between the consideration paid and the fair value of identifiable net assets is recognized as goodwill. As for the goodwill already on Haitong Securities' books, the principle explicitly stated in the SSE review inquiry response was that it would not be considered when recognizing the acquired identifiable assets and liabilities — in other words, Haitong Securities' existing goodwill was zeroed out in the combination, and new goodwill was recalculated based on the current fair value differential.

Comparing these two transactions side by side sharpens one's understanding of how "common control" is determined. Both mergers were launched almost simultaneously, were comparable in scale and similar in nature — CSSC + CSIC was valued at RMB 115.15 billion, Guotai Junan + Haitong Securities was also in the hundreds-of-billions range, and both were state-directed same-industry consolidations. Yet because of a single difference — whether the parent groups had merged five years earlier — a difference seemingly unrelated to the current transaction, the accounting outcomes diverged completely: one produced zero goodwill, the other produced massive goodwill; one permitted retrospective restatement, the other did not.

This is the true meaning of the "common control" determination in the Chinese context — it is not a question of "who substantively controls," but a question of "whether the entities are formally within the same enterprise group." The same state directive, the same consolidation purpose, the same state-owned capital background — merely the presence or absence of a preceding organizational step can produce entirely different accounting outcomes.

#### Contractual Control

The third case is about an accounting rule being used in a scenario it was never designed to serve.

On April 13, 2000, Sina Corporation went public on NASDAQ. In that IPO, Sina pioneered a corporate structure that would later become known as the "VIE structure": the offshore listing entity (incorporated in the Cayman Islands) exercised control over a domestic Chinese operating company through a series of agreements — an exclusive technical services agreement, an equity pledge agreement, an exclusive option agreement, and a voting rights proxy agreement — rather than through equity ownership. This structure allowed offshore investors to indirectly share in the economic benefits of Chinese domestic internet businesses while circumventing China's restrictions on foreign investment in the telecommunications and internet sectors.

A timing note is needed here: when Sina went public in 2000, the name "VIE" did not yet exist — the structure was known in the industry as the "Sina Structure" or "contractual control structure." "VIE" as an accounting term did not emerge until December 2003, when FASB issued FIN 46(R). FIN 46(R) was one of FASB's responses to the 2001 Enron scandal — Enron had set up numerous ostensibly independent shell companies to hide liabilities and high-risk assets off its consolidated balance sheet. To close this loophole, FASB introduced the concept of the "Variable Interest Entity" (VIE): even if an entity appears to be owned by independent external shareholders in its equity structure, if its economic risks and rewards are substantively borne by another party, that party must consolidate it.

FIN 46(R) was intended to pull "formally independent but substantively controlled" entities back onto the balance sheet — an anti-avoidance, loophole-closing rule. It had no direct connection to Sina's 2000 IPO; U.S. GAAP before 2003 also allowed consolidation of domestic operating companies based on the general principle of "substantive control," just without a unified accounting term. After FIN 46(R) was issued, structures that "achieve control through agreements rather than equity" were formally classified under the VIE framework, and the term "VIE structure" was retroactively applied to early cases like Sina.

Chinese internet companies used VIEs for the exact opposite purpose of what FASB intended. FASB created the concept to pull back shell companies that were trying to "escape consolidation"; Chinese internet companies used VIE in reverse — what they wanted was precisely "proactive consolidation." Only by enabling the offshore listing entity to consolidate the domestic operating company could overseas investors see complete financial information and could the IPO proceed. They applied the same principle from FIN 46(R) — "substantive control requires consolidation" — but in the opposite direction: FASB was guarding against companies using shells to hide assets, while VIE structures were using agreements to "pull domestic assets in from outside for consolidation."

Over the following two decades, the VIE structure became the standard tool for Chinese internet companies listing overseas. Sina, Sohu, NetEase, Baidu, Alibaba, JD.com, and New Oriental listed in the U.S.; Tencent listed in Hong Kong — virtually all of that generation's prominent internet companies built their offshore listings on this structure. In September 2014, Alibaba listed on the New York Stock Exchange. After exercising the over-allotment option, total proceeds reached approximately $25 billion, setting the record for the largest IPO in global history at that time (a record later broken by Saudi Aramco in 2019). The entire transaction's legal and accounting foundation was the VIE.

But the VIE has a particular fragility. The "Alipay Incident" of 2011 exposed it.

In June 2010, the People's Bank of China (PBOC) issued the *Administrative Measures for Payment Services of Non-Financial Institutions*, stipulating that third-party payment licenses must be held by domestically funded enterprises, and companies with foreign ownership components (including contractual control) needed separate State Council approval. Alipay was at that time indirectly controlled by Alibaba Group (incorporated in the Cayman Islands) through a VIE structure, which under the new rules no longer qualified as "purely domestically funded."

To ensure Alipay could obtain a payment license in the first batch, Jack Ma carried out a series of actions to strip Alipay out of the VIE structure, roughly in three steps: in June 2009, 70% of Alipay's equity was transferred from Alibaba Group's Cayman subsidiary Alipay to Zhejiang Alibaba E-Commerce Co., Ltd., a company 80% owned by Jack Ma personally; in August 2010, the remaining 30% was also transferred to Zhejiang Alibaba. When these two steps were completed, Zhejiang Alibaba itself was still under Alibaba Group's VIE contractual control, so Alipay remained within Alibaba Group's consolidation scope in accounting terms. The truly critical move was the third step — in Q1 2011, Jack Ma unilaterally terminated Alibaba Group's VIE contractual control over Zhejiang Alibaba. Once this step was completed, Alipay was fully removed from Alibaba Group's consolidation scope, finally satisfying the PBOC's "purely domestically funded" requirement.

The third step was taken without the explicit prior consent of Alibaba Group's board — at the time, the major shareholders were Yahoo and SoftBank — triggering a fierce dispute among shareholders. In July 2011, the three parties signed a compensation agreement stipulating that when Alipay eventually went public, it would make a one-time cash payment of $2 billion to $6 billion to Alibaba Group. In 2014, the three parties revised the agreement again, allowing Alibaba to convert this "future IPO cash payment" into equity in Ant Financial (the financial services group that Alipay then belonged to). In September 2019, Alibaba exercised this option, acquiring 33% of newly issued Ant Financial shares, while the previous arrangement under which Ant Financial paid Alibaba an annual fee of "37.5% of pre-tax profit" for intellectual property and technical services was terminated — Alibaba recognized a one-time gain of approximately RMB 69.2 billion from this equity swap. From Alibaba's perspective, the loss from the 2011 "unilateral taking of Alipay" was eventually, after eight years of back-and-forth negotiations and two agreement revisions, converted into a one-third equity stake in Ant Financial.

The Alipay incident laid bare a fundamental problem with VIEs.

Under a VIE structure, "control" is not equity control but contractual control. The enforceability of contracts depends on two things: the integrity of the actual controller, and the feasibility of judicial remedies. In the Alipay case, neither could be relied upon — the actual controller made a unilateral decision, and suing over the validity of VIE agreements in Chinese courts was itself a grey area, because VIE structures involve circumvention of foreign investment restrictions and the judicial enforceability of the relevant contracts had never been clearly affirmed. Overseas shareholders had virtually no reliable legal remedies.

Under an ordinary equity structure, actual controllers can also harm minority shareholders' interests, but at least ordinary equity is clear-cut — ownership is registered, and the law's definition of shareholder rights is unambiguous. VIEs are different: control is built entirely on a set of contracts whose own legal status remains unresolved in judicial practice. Once the actual controller decides to act unilaterally, overseas shareholders have almost no corresponding remedies. This is the most fundamental fragility of the VIE as a "consolidation tool" — not an accounting fragility, but an institutional one.

The accounting rules themselves were not circumvented; the VIE's accounting treatment fully complied with the intent of FIN 46(R) — substantive control requires consolidation. What was used unexpectedly was the commercial context in which the rule operated: FASB originally envisioned the companies subject to VIE rules as those trying to escape consolidation, but in the Chinese market, VIE became a compliance tool for proactively pursuing consolidation. This outcome was neither FASB's intent nor formally endorsed by any regulator, yet it has in practice shaped the capital structure of China's internet industry for over two decades.

## How Investors Should Read a Company's Consolidated Statements

Returning to the investor's perspective. When reading a company's consolidated financial statements, what requires attention is not just the statements themselves, but several "boundary signals." No single signal constitutes conclusive evidence, but each is worth using as a starting point for further inquiry.

**Minority interest.** If a company's minority interest as a share of total equity is significantly higher than minority shareholders' profit or loss as a share of net income — say the former is 60% while the latter is only 10% — this structural imbalance deserves serious suspicion. But this metric alone is not proof. A genuine minority-owned subsidiary that is temporarily loss-making or in an investment phase can also produce a mismatch between the equity and profit ratios. The key is whether the imbalance persists over multiple years, whether it exists across different project companies, and whether it is accompanied by large related-party transactions or controlling shareholder guarantees — only when multiple signals appear simultaneously does the probability of equity-in-name-debt-in-substance become high.

**Fund transfer channels.** If a company consistently channels funds to unconsolidated related parties or obscure private companies through various line items such as "capital increases," "loans," "prepayments," and "other receivables," this warrants suspicion and vigilance. Sophisticated fraud operators will even execute a "round-trip" of these balances before the balance sheet date, making the period-end figures look unremarkable, only to push the funds back out at the start of the next period. Looking at period-end balances alone can easily miss the signals; what really matters is the flow of transactions and gross amounts, especially large movements around period-end dates. In the years before LeEco's collapse, it continuously transferred funds to related parties in its unlisted ecosystem through long-term equity investments, other receivables, and prepayments — a textbook example of this pattern.

## Conclusion

Corporate management will always know the control boundaries of their own companies better than standard-setters, and will always find ways to make the language of standards serve their consolidation objectives. Auditors are supposed to be the first external line of defense, but in practice auditors can be misled by management, softened by the "everyone does it" industry norm, and sometimes willing to turn a blind eye to borderline judgments to retain a client.

Standards are not omnipotent, and neither are auditors. Investors need to stay vigilant on their own. When reading consolidated financial statements, look not only at the numbers but at the boundaries behind them — are there entities that should not have been excluded from the consolidation scope? Has the "common control" classification been used to bury pricing information that should appear as goodwill into capital reserves? Does the minority interest line contain items that are liabilities in substance? Are long-term equity investments and other receivables being used as disguised fund transfer channels?

Consolidated financial statements are a rough outline of a company, but the company's true shape is sometimes much larger than what the outline shows — and sometimes it looks entirely different.
