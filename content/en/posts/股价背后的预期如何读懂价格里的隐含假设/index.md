A listed company reports clearly strong results, yet the stock doesn't rise—or even falls. Many people have probably run into this kind of confusion. The answer lies in one word: "expectations." A stock price has never reflected the present; it reflects the market's expectations for the future. No matter how good the results are, if they merely deliver on the expectations already baked into the price, they can't push the stock higher. What truly moves a stock is a change in the expectations themselves. For the individual investor, understanding "expectations" is more or less the starting point for understanding stock prices.

## Where Do Expectations Come From

So where do expectations come from? A stock price isn't set by any single person, and these expectations clearly aren't those of any single institution either. Is there such a thing as a "market consensus expectation"? In fact, there isn't—but we can often aggregate the expectations of many analysts and arrive at an average that serves as the market's overall expectation. This is what we frequently see on various investment platforms, usually as two metrics: expected revenue and expected net profit.

Although it is free cash flow that determines a company's intrinsic value, market analysts tend to focus more on the company's earnings over the next year or two, and use multiples such as the P/E ratio to derive a valuation. In fact, it is precisely analysts' earnings forecasts that drive the stock price: when a company's earnings announcement or guidance deviates from analysts' forecasts, the stock price reacts accordingly. This is what we commonly call a "beat" or a "miss."

## Stock Prices Don't Always Reflect Market Expectations

But Mr. Market isn't always so rational. While most of the time his quote reflects the market's expectations for a company's future results, once his mood swings to an extreme—extreme optimism or extreme pessimism—the stock price will detach from the market's overall expectations.

Tencent is a ready-made example right now. Since the start of 2026, its stock has trended downward overall, falling from over HK$600 at the beginning of the year to around HK$420 at one point. Yet even using the current market consensus for revenue and profit, its forward P/E multiple is already very low—not to mention that its balance sheet still holds over a trillion in equity investments; strip those out, and the "true" P/E corresponding to the core business is even lower. The fair target prices given by analysts are also generally far above the current price. By rights this is a cheap price, yet it just keeps falling.

When a stock falls, all kinds of rationalizing narratives appear—for instance, that Tencent lags in AI progress, that future AI capital expenditures will be too large, and so on. Many people will say that Tencent's current stock price reflects exactly these pessimistic expectations. But be careful: the stock market is never short of narratives, and much of the time they are merely imagination, or simply a way to rationalize the stock's fluctuations—while the market consensus revenue and net profit have not been revised as a result. Once the earnings-announcement window arrives, everyone again picks up expected revenue and net profit as the measuring stick, and at that point, beating or missing market expectations once again becomes the catalyst that determines where the stock goes.

So, can we make buy and sell decisions based on the analyst consensus and target prices?

Target prices can serve as a reference, but you must see their limitations clearly. First, almost everything we can see comes from sell-side analyst reports—buy-side reports are for that firm's own investors, even a kind of privilege, and won't be made public to the market. Second, market analysts always tend to focus on short-term earnings and valuation multiples, so the target prices they give are easily swayed by market sentiment: when the market is overheated, the valuation multiples they assign can be revised upward too; when there's panic, the reverse.

Since stock prices don't always reflect market expectations, how should we understand the implied expectations in Mr. Market's quote? And since analyst expectations and target prices aren't always reliable either, how do we find an anchor for whether a price is reasonable?

## Back to Intrinsic Value

As value investors, if we want to survive in this market over the long term and want to hold onto our stocks, we cannot be swayed by the market's short-term sentiment and momentum. Ultimately, we must return to the company's intrinsic value—that is, free cash flow. As I emphasized in a previous article, free-cash-flow valuation is the first principle of value investing.

Many people dislike using free-cash-flow valuation, because it involves many parameters and complex calculations, and because it involves subjective judgment, making it hard to arrive at a precise valuation. This view isn't wrong. But the crux of the problem lies not in the tool itself, but in how we use it. My takeaways are twofold: insist on being roughly right, and learn to reason in reverse.

First, insist on being roughly right. As described in a previous article, we needn't pursue precise calculation; better to be roughly right than precisely wrong. Methodologically, use simple forecasts and minimize parameter estimation as much as possible, provided this doesn't materially sacrifice the quality of the analysis. Take cash flow forecasting: the traditional approach requires building three statements, and in particular calculating changes in working capital by linking the balance sheet and the income statement. But changes in working capital and fixed-asset investment are actually both part of reinvestment, and can perfectly well be combined and inferred through their ratio relationship to other metrics (primarily revenue or operating profit). This is the advantage of Damodaran's valuation approach.

## Reasoning in Reverse: Understanding the Expectations Implied in the Price

Second, learn to reason in reverse. Investors are all in dialogue with Mr. Market; what we have to do is choose whether to accept or reject the quote he offers. How do we know whether that quote is reasonable? We can use a free-cash-flow valuation model to reverse-engineer the expectations for the core operating variables implied in the current quote (Price-Implied Expectations).

This is essentially using the valuation model from a different angle: a forward DCF uses the model to calculate value, asking "what is it actually worth"; a reverse DCF uses the model to understand the operating expectations implied in the market's pricing, asking "what must I believe for today's price to hold," and then compares these implied expectation values with one's own judgment. It should be noted that the "reverse solution" here is done based on a free-cash-flow valuation model, on the premise that you agree—that the core measure of a company's value is its intrinsic value.

What are the key implied operating variables in reverse reasoning? As I've also analyzed in a previous article, starting from the essence of running a business, there are two core variables: revenue growth and operating margin—one about growth, one about efficiency. Revenue growth and operating margin together determine the core metric for calculating free cash flow: net operating profit after tax (NOPAT).

Beyond that, the reinvestment rate and the cost of capital (WACC) are also important variables that need to be estimated, but they can be handled in a simplified way. For many companies the historical reinvestment-rate data is very stable, and can often be inferred from history; as for WACC or the discount rate, although there is a clear calculation formula, every investor has his own risk tolerance and required rate of return, so it can perfectly well be placed in scenario analysis—simply take the hurdle rate of return you can accept, rather than treating it as a core variable in studying the expectations behind a stock price.

## Growth and Efficiency Are Not a Linear Relationship

Between revenue growth and operating margin there exists a subtle relationship: they do not grow in lockstep in a linear fashion. This is precisely why using linear-extrapolation thinking to understand a company's growth and valuation is so often wrong. Two important forces determine this relationship.

One force comes from the company's own business attributes—namely, economies of scale. You could say economies of scale are the "universal gravitation" of the business world; one important source of imagination that makes a company worth investing in lies precisely in how economies of scale give it the potential to grow bigger and stronger. There are two core sources of economies of scale: one is what finance calls operating leverage, based on the impact of fixed costs—as revenue grows, operating profit improves rapidly and grows at a faster rate. This is the root of why a company's margins keep climbing with scale and the two exhibit a non-linear relationship. The other is the increasingly important network effect in modern business: as a platform grows larger, the demand side generates greater economies of scale.

The other force comes from outside—namely, market competition. Take, for instance, China's current "involuted" (neijuan) economic environment, where the result is that we frequently see companies grow revenue without growing profit—the efficiency gains brought by scale are forced to be ceded to customers through price cuts, just to maintain that growth. The key to assessing the strength of this external force lies in understanding the company's moat: if the moat is wider and pricing power in the market is stronger, the company is less susceptible to external competition, and the profit generated by operating leverage can truly be retained and realized as expanding margins.

## How to Reverse-Engineer the Two Core Variables

Since free-cash-flow valuation involves two core variables behind it, analyzing them is essentially an exercise in sensitivity analysis—searching, along different combinations of growth rate and margin, for the various combinations of variables that support the current stock price. The DCF valuation provided by valuescope.app comes with exactly this kind of sensitivity analysis, and much of the time this is how people use it. Below I'd like to offer another approach: how, based on an analysis of the drivers of the relationship between the two variables of growth and efficiency, to further simplify the reverse reasoning—converting these two variables into one, or at least highlighting one of them—so that the complexity of understanding the expectations implied behind a stock price is reduced even further.

How exactly to do this? First, assess this company: is its operating margin stable? Is the impact of external competition large? If a company's operating margin has always been very stable and the impact of external competition is small, we can treat the operating margin as a stable value, and then focus on the implied expectation for revenue growth. Take Kweichow Moutai, for example: its historical operating margin has been extremely robust, and it has a strong brand effect and pricing power; although growth has slowed somewhat in recent years, its margin remains highly stable with very little fluctuation—making it well suited to reading the price in this "lock the margin, solve only for growth" manner.

Moutai is the most ideal case. But in reality, for more companies revenue growth and operating margin are not mutually independent—rather, there is a clear correlation between them—and in that case you cannot simply "lock" the margin, but must handle it by following the relationship between the two. There are two types of situations that call for special caution.

One is where there is clear convexity between the two—that is, companies with very strong economies of scale. For these companies the margin is not a constant, but rises continuously as revenue grows—the classic example being early-stage platform or software companies, whose current margin may be very low or even loss-making, but that is often because customer acquisition, R&D, and other investments are heavily expensed, depressing current-period profit, and does not represent their true earning power. These companies' businesses have very strong economies of scale, and margins will climb quickly along with revenue growth, but with a ceiling determined by the cost structure (roughly its contribution margin). Reverse reasoning can focus on the revenue growth implied behind the price, and then concentrate on testing whether the margin-improvement path is worthy of the implied growth: can operating leverage support such a steep margin expansion? Has it crossed the ceiling set by the cost structure? Many overvalued companies are precisely those where the market simultaneously assumes high growth, sharp margin expansion, and no increase in investment—three things that, by economic laws, simply cannot hold true at the same time.

The other is the situation of fierce external competition where it's easy to grow revenue without growing profit—growth often has to be bought with concessions, revenue rises, yet the margin may stagnate or even be eroded. For these companies, the danger isn't growth, but whether the margin can hold; so the core variable to highlight here is precisely the margin, not growth: you should stress-test using a more conservative margin that doesn't expand or even declines. The thing to be most wary of is the market treating "high growth" and "margin expansion" as things that can be obtained at the same time—whether both can be had in an involuted landscape depends on the moat. Only if the moat is wide enough can you even speak of having both growth and margin.

In the end, reverse reasoning lets us handle DCF valuation from a different angle: not using the model to pursue a precise price calculation—that kind of precision is inherently subjective—but to understand the true expectations implied behind the market's quote, and then to compare and test them against business common sense and our own knowledge. What growth expectations and operating efficiency does the stock price reflect for this company? Are these implied operating variables reasonable? Only when you can articulate where the market might be wrong, and the expectations implied in the price significantly diverge from your own judgment and knowledge, does an investment opportunity truly emerge. This is also the best test we have for avoiding paying too high a price for a stock.
